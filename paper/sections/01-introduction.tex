\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The concept of a \emph{security ceremony} expands the idea of a security protocol to include the human participants who engage in it~\cite{ellison2007ceremony}. A rigorous treatment of a ceremony must consider how people behave in practice, which involves the mistakes they make~\cite{bella2012layered,basin2016modeling,bella2022modelling,fila2024nothing}. The methodology referred to as \XMen{}~\cite{sempreboni2020x,sempreboni2023mutation} represents these human mistakes as \emph{mutations} applied to a formal ceremony model: a specification that has been mutated describes a person who is deviating from the correct procedure, its consequences are identified and carried through to other participants, and an unchanged verification tool, Tamarin~\cite{meier2013tamarin,basin2025modeling}, scrutinises it just as it would analyse the unmutated ceremony. The contributions of Sandu et al.~\SCP{} introduced a mutation named \forget{}, designed to capture memory failures. Information that is forgotten is not eliminated from the human's knowledge base, which continues to accumulate; it is noted down in a distinct \Forget{} collection, and for every sending action performed by the human, the mutation determines whether the content of the intended message can still be constructed without relying on forgotten premises. Based on that determination, the send operation may be preserved, \emph{swapped} for an alternative message the human is still able to formulate, or \emph{omitted} entirely; a memory lapse that influences an internal verification step results in that check being \emph{ignored}. This technique uncovers attacks that stem from forgetfulness rather than cryptographic weaknesses, for example a scenario where a user has forgotten the password for one bank and consequently transmits a password for a different bank.

The approach taken by \Forget{} relies on a syntactic test, one we term \emph{set-based}: any derivation becomes unusable if any one of its premises is blocked by the \Forget{} set. Three key ceremony-related phenomena, however, are not captured by it. To begin with, participants lose skills, not just facts: a user might hold every value a step requires yet still fail to execute it, because what was forgotten is a procedure, not a piece of data~\cite{ryle1949concept,cohen1980preserved}. Next, forgetting frequently stems from a failure of access, not loss: a prompt, like a password reminder, can render a forgotten value available for a specific step without that value being relearnt~\cite{tulving1974cue,tulving1973encoding}. Finally, the test yields a verdict without explanation, meaning an analyst who observes a \replace{} cannot determine which lapse triggered it or whether a cue could have averted it. None of these phenomena constitutes an extra element of the \Forget{} set. Instead, they are relationships among pieces of information that work against each other: ordinarily, a known value can be employed; a lapse is a departure from that norm; a cue is a further departure that overrides the lapse.

\paragraph{This paper.} We construct a \emph{defeasible reasoning framework} for \forget{}. For every human transition, the person's cognitive state is translated into a theory of the ambiguity-blocking, team-defeat Defeasible Logic DL($\partial$)~\cite{nute1994defeasible,antoniou2001representation}. Knowing ($\Known$) and being able to use ($\Usable$) are distinct atoms; every routine derivation of a candidate message is a labeled conclusion ($\Via$); a lapse concerning a value, an inference rule, or an entire derivation acts as a defeater against that conclusion; and a cue is a higher-priority rule that cancels the defeater for one premise while the lapse remains noted. The mutation is extracted from the proof tags of the $\Usable$ atoms, and the proof supplies the explanation.

This work presents a novel redefinition of the \forget{} mutation, enabling us to set up, verify, and advance it. Employing a stricter logical structure, we describe precisely the outcomes of the compiled theories and demonstrate that these depend on clear, specific choices in their proof foundations: rescuing multiple failures is possible with team defeat but impossible without it, whereas both ambiguity blocking and propagation shows identical results for every transformed system. We establish that the system widens the set-based semantics conservatively and cleanly divides conditions that the \Forget{} set cannot tell apart, that choices remain reliable even when inference is truncated, and that usability can be settled efficiently unless there is a forget of an exact derivation. The study further reveals the system's limits: within its systems, a failure can only be overridden, and no principle determines that an inference is unusable. Adversarial cues, which disrupt a proper value, cross this boundary: usability ceases to increase steadily with the cues available, and the selection of a defeasible semantics determines whether a person encountering contradictory cues proceeds with a different option or abstains.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A defeasible reasoning framework for \forget{} (\cref{sec:framework}): a cognitive state containing procedural lapses and cues, its transformation into DL($\partial$), and a method for making choices accompanied by reasons and clear coverage results.
\item A theoretical examination of the defeasible framework (\cref{sec:properties}): a precise description of the compiled theories, how they rely on team defeat and do not rely on the ambiguity approach, a conservative extension of~\SCP{} along with two separations, soundness and bounded completeness under bounded enumeration, and a description of usability solvable by a fast algorithm.
\item A concrete version in the \XMen~2.0 program and its assessment (\cref{sec:implementation,sec:evaluation}): comparative testing against another, independent encoding of DL($\partial$), which revealed a flaw in team defeat in that other coding; and Tamarin experiments where the fresh choices alter verification results.
\item An initial, not yet built, representation of adversarial cues (\cref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}
The \forget{} mutation, the \Forget{} set, its blocked hypotheses and the set-based semantics originate from~\SCP{} (\cref{sec:background}); everything else in the document is original.
