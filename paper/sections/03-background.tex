\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Messages and derivations}
Messages $\Msgs$ are terms of the free algebra over atoms (names, fresh values, public constants), pairing $\pair{a}{b}$, symmetric encryption $\senc{m}{k}$, asymmetric encryption $\aenc{m}{\pk{s}}$, and public and private function symbols. The inference rules $\RR$ are the usual Dolev--Yao rules~\cite{dolev1983security}: the constructors $\lbl{pair}$, $\lbl{senc}$, $\lbl{aenc}$ and one constructor for each public function symbol, and the destructors $\lbl{proj}$ (both projections), $\lbl{sdec}$ (premises: ciphertext and key) and $\lbl{adec}$ (premises: ciphertext and private key). The first premise of a destructor is its \emph{principal} premise.

A \emph{derivation} $\pi$ of $t$ from a finite $K\subseteq\Msgs$ is a finite tree whose leaves are elements of $K$, whose inner nodes apply rules of $\RR$, and whose root is $t$. Its \emph{hypotheses} $\Hyp_\pi\subseteq K$ are its leaves and $\Rules(\pi)\subseteq\RR$ is the set of rules it applies. A derivation is \emph{normal} if no destructor's principal premise is the conclusion of the inverse constructor and no constructor rebuilds a term from the outputs of its inverse destructor~\SCP. $\Norm_t(K)$ is the set of normal derivations of $t$ from $K$.

\begin{remark}[Finite basis]\label{rem:finite}
$\Norm_t(K)$ can be infinite: for $K=\{\senc{a}{b},$ $\senc{b}{a},$ $a\}$, $a$ is obtained by decrypting $\senc{a}{b}$ with $b$, which is obtained by decrypting $\senc{b}{a}$ with $a$, and so on. This corrects the statement in~\SCP{} that the space of normal derivations is finite. We call $\Basis^*_t(K)\subseteq\Norm_t(K)$, the normal derivations in which no message occurs twice on a root-to-leaf path, the \emph{finite basis}: every message on such a path is a subterm of $K\cup\{t\}$, so $\Basis^*_t(K)$ is finite.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Forget mutation~\SCP}
A human role is a sequence of transitions $\AgSt(\human,i,\K{\human}{i}),\ \mathit{Rcv}(m_1) \xrightarrow{a} \AgSt(\human,i+1,\K{\human}{i+1}),\ \mathit{Snd}(m_2)$, where $\K{\human}{i}$ is the human's (monotone) knowledge and $a$ contains internal actions such as checks. \forget{} records forgotten messages in a set $\Forget^i_\human$. Whether a hypothesis $\bar m$ is \emph{blocked} depends on a closure policy $\kappa$: under Case~1, $\bar m\in\Forget$; under Case~2, $\bar m$ is derivable from $\Forget$ with the pairing rules; under Case~3, with all Dolev--Yao rules. $\Blocked(X,\Forget)$ is the set of blocked elements of $X$. Blocking is pointwise: whether $\bar m$ is blocked depends on $\bar m$, $\Forget$ and $\kappa$ only. A message $t$ is \emph{derivable under forgetting} if some derivation $\pi$ of $t$ has $\Blocked(\Hyp_\pi,\Forget)=\varnothing$~\cite[Def.~4]{sandu2026formal}.

The \emph{published algorithm}~\SCP{} works per transition. A forgotten known message is added to $\Forget$; a forgotten received $m_1$ is not added to the knowledge. If the forgotten message occurs in an internal action, the action is removed (\neglect). Otherwise, if some derivation of $m_2$ has no blocked hypothesis, $m_2$ is sent; if not, for each derivation and blocked hypothesis $\bar m$ it collects the set $R_{\bar m}$ of known, non-forgotten messages with the same format and type label, and emits one \replace{} per combination of replacements, or \skipm{} if there is none. Re-receiving a forgotten message, or deriving it from a received one, removes it from $\Forget$.

\subsection{Defeasible Logic}
A defeasible theory $\Dth=(F,R,>)$ has a finite set $F$ of literals (facts), a finite set $R$ of labelled rules $r: A(r)\hookrightarrow C(r)$ that are strict ($\strict$), defeasible ($\defeas$) or defeaters ($\defeat$), and an acyclic superiority relation $>$ on rules with complementary heads~\cite{nute1994defeasible,antoniou2001representation}. A defeater cannot support its head; it can only prevent the complementary conclusion. We write $R[q]$ for the rules with head $q$, $R_s[q]$ and $\Rsd[q]$ for its strict, and strict or defeasible, rules, and ${\sim}q$ for the complement of $q$.
We use the ambiguity-blocking logic DL($\partial$) with \emph{team defeat}~\cite{antoniou2001representation}. Its conclusions are tagged: $\pD q$ and $\mD q$ for definite (strict) provability and its refutation, and
\begin{align*}
\pd q \iff{}& \pD q \text{ or } \bigl[\exists r\in\Rsd[q]\ \forall a\in A(r){:}\ \pd a\bigr] \wedge \mD{\sim}q \wedge \forall s\in R[{\sim}q]{:}\\
&\quad \bigl(\exists a\in A(s){:}\ \md a\bigr) \vee \bigl(\exists t\in\Rsd[q]{:}\ \forall a\in A(t){:}\ \pd a \wedge t>s\bigr),
\end{align*}
with $\md q$ defined as its strong negation (\cref{app:variants}). A rule is \emph{applicable} if all its antecedents are $\pd$, and \emph{discarded} if one is $\md$. Two features matter below. \emph{Team defeat}: each attacker $s$ of $q$ may be overruled by a \emph{different} applicable supporter $t$. \emph{Ambiguity blocking}: a literal whose support is tied with its attack receives $\md$, and rules depending on it are discarded. The two alternatives, individual defeat~\cite{billington1993defeasible} and ambiguity propagation~\cite{antoniou2000flexible,governatori2004argumentation}, are defined in \cref{app:variants}. Note that $\md q$ is a proved conclusion: it differs from the failure to prove $\pd q$ and from $\pd{\sim}q$.
